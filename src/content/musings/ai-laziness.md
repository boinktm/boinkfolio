There is a pervasive narrative floating around right now that suggests if you use Artificial Intelligence, you are somehow bypassing the learning process. It suggests that by asking a machine for an answer, or by delegating the execution of code to an agent, you are becoming "lazy." People say this with varying degrees of conviction—some with genuine concern for their children's education, others with a sort of moral superiority about how work should be done. But when I look at my own life, and when I look at the people around me who hold these views, I find that their definition of laziness is fundamentally flawed because it confuses methodology with outcome.

To understand where this argument goes wrong, we have to start with what "research" actually means. My wife recently had a disagreement with me over something trivial—I used Grok to figure out how many Yakult shots were in a serving after she drank an entire six-pack. She derided the act of using the tool, arguing that I wasn't "actually researching." Her stance was that the mere act of sorting through various sources and coming to a personal conclusion was the research itself. But this is where her understanding breaks down. In the modern information age, searching for an answer rarely yields a definitive truth. You do not simply Google something and get the answer; you get Source 1 which contradicts Source 2, while Source 3 introduces a new element or context that doesn't apply to your specific situation. Eventually, you have a wide array of information that is contradictory, often based on anecdotes from Reddit threads rather than data. You develop a conclusion based on the sum of what you have absorbed, but you are also absorbing noise, misinformation, and irrelevance along the way. Using AI in this context isn't avoiding work; it is filtering signal from noise. It allows me to get the factual baseline I need so that I can focus my mental energy on using that information rather than finding it. If "research" means wading through mud because I was told that muddiness makes me stronger, then yes, I am choosing not to do that. But calling that laziness is a category error.

This brings us to the more aggressive stance held by my brother, who is adamantly anti-AI in his pursuit of a traditional computer science degree. He views AI usage as a crutch that prevents one from learning or performing research properly. I respect his dedication; he has spent years immersing himself in the fundamentals of IT, but his view seems rooted in a time period where the world stopped moving around 2019. He believes that if I am not typing every line myself, I cannot understand my codebase. While it is true that he may understand the fundamentals of his specific codebase better than I do regarding low-level syntax, this obsession with "struggle" as a virtue ignores the reality of what we are actually trying to achieve.

I have spent from the age of 16 until now at 28 with my hands on computers. I engaged in many hobbies: game development, music production, web design, 3D modeling. And for years, I bounced off these pursuits hundreds of times. I would spend quite a lot of money and time taking courses—Udemy, Google courses, Python, C++—but usually by my own fault, some sort of friction happened that caused me to bounce off. I retained none of it. The barrier wasn't a lack of intelligence; the barrier was the sheer weight of the learning curve combined with outdated educational models. In high school, for example, we took an AP Programming class where we spent the entire course reading the biography of the guy who wrote Java, but we didn't actually learn how to write Java in a meaningful way. A separate web design class taught us barebone HTML and CSS that could have been pulled straight from 2002. We were learning things that had no relevance for our future because there was never a need for me to keep up with what was new or what I should actually be learning.

This is where the "laziness" accusation falls apart completely. Before AI, my default path was inaction. It wasn't laziness in the sense of doing nothing; it was paralysis through friction. The idea would fade into existence because I couldn't bridge the gap between the vision and the execution fast enough. Now, with AI, I am able to create websites and programs that are more feature-rich in exponentially less time than my brother can manage. Many times, I simply forgo telling him of any AI usage, and he is sufficiently impressed every time. The truth is, I have never been as productive, nor have I ever released as many projects as I have since the age of AI. That is the complete opposite of laziness.

When we talk about productivity, we have to look at the Return on Investment (ROI) of our effort. There are certain things I simply would not have done if it wasn't for AI, purely because the ROI isn't worth it. For example, I made a fully featured Pokedex in the web browser over this weekend. Sure, I could have spent a month learning JavaScript, studying the PokeAPI, and getting up to speed on what Tailwind, Astro, and Bootstrap are. But would I have retained that info three years from now? Would it even be relevant three months from now with how fast things are progressing? Am I willing to dedicate myself to loving "the code" so much that I become knowledgeable enough to become employed just for the sake of knowing the syntax? The truth, in my case given the life upswing I am in, is frankly no.

I have entered a whole new career field and been promoted as high as I can go for now because I am utilizing these tools to catch up on what I need to know now, rather than what was important five years ago. In my hobby of creating Call of Duty: Black Ops 3 maps, this is even more apparent. For years, I would spend hours spinning the camera around or looking at the 3D space for things to do, paralyzed by where to start. Some of my peers are outputting a fraction of the content others of my caliber could perform in a similar time because they are bogged down by the same friction. When I want to tackle a personal project that I feel I would never have had the time for, I enter a peak flow state as I guide the agents and perform what I feel are the actual creative parts, just in a non-direct way. My 7th map is almost entirely scripted using agentic coding. People are excited to try it. Some will ask questions about how it was made, but the people who matter—the players—care that the experience works, not whether I typed the GSC code manually in 2016 or today.

So when critics say I am lazy for using AI, they are looking at the wrong metric. They define laziness as "not struggling with the tool." But I define laziness as "letting the idea fade into existence" or hoping that someone more capable has already had it. The reason why people think letting ideas fade isn't a sin is because no one would call you lazy for simply not producing, provided you are a productive member of society in other ways. But when you do produce, and you use AI to do it, the accusation of laziness is an attempt to police how you achieved that production rather than celebrating the fact that you achieved it at all.

I don't put stock into what others have to say about praise or criticism because I feel like the only person who can understand the breadth of what a person does and what it means to them is that person themselves. I enjoy the pride I feel in simply having stuff to show off far more than the anguish I would feel having half-baked, unreleased projects that would eventually find their way into the garbage bin. My identity isn't tied to whether my websites were made with AI or by hand; who I am is not the equation of those things. I am known for being dependable, kind, hardworking, and loving. Those traits are what define me in my personal life, regardless of the tools used to build a website.

In conclusion, the accusation that AI makes people lazy ignores the reality of how we function as humans today. We are not defined by our manual labor; we are defined by our direction. Using AI allows us to bypass the obsolete parts of learning—the rote memorization of syntax that changes every year—and focus on the creative and strategic decisions that actually require a human mind. It lowers the activation energy required to start, preventing the burnout that comes from staring at a screen for hours trying to debug a problem you already know how to solve conceptually. If anything, AI has turned me into someone who ships work. And if "laziness" is defined by inaction and letting ideas die because of friction, then using AI isn't laziness; it's the only way I have found to be truly ambitious. We are not losing our humanity by using tools; we are finally freeing ourselves from the grind that keeps us from doing what actually matters.
